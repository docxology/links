Applied Regenerative Economics

Mapping Environmental Web3

Louise Borreani
9 min read
·
Oct 11, 2021

--

Blockchain and the environment are two poorly associated terms. Today, Bitcoin’s apocalyptic energy consumption steals headlines, drawing critical attention away from Web3’s potential to disrupt and innovate the environmental sector. We believe it is high time to dismantle this assumption, by defining the emerging ecosystem making use of Web3 technologies for environmental purposes. In this blog, I map out and shine the needed light towards these burgeoning efforts.

In opposition to Web2 — the internet that most of society knows and uses today — Web3 provides a decentralized, holistic way to approach the Internet, using blockchains and other distributed ledger technologies (DLTs). According to this article by Fabric Ventures, Web3’s inherent characteristics include:

  • Openness — applications are open-source and transparent for everyone.
  • Trustless — trust between different parties is unnecessary to interact.
  • Permissionless — anybody can access and utilize Web3 without authorization.

In response to climate change’s growing threat, scholars and researchers are focusing increasingly on representing and modeling the interlinkages and interactions between the human and the biosphere. Fields of study, such as regenerative economics, adaptive governance, socioecological systems, and ecological economics are all products of this combined effort. With Web3’s emergence, new tooling is now freely available for these fields to cross the chasm from theory to pilot implementations — which many projects now pursue.

Blockchain’s Fructuous Energy Sector

At first glance, when examining environmental blockchain, it is apparent that the energy sector is further developed than other sector categories. Blockchain naturally addresses key energy sector issues related to provenance, data management, and resource allocation. As such, major DLT investments have already been made in this domain. Energy innovation is in demand and is generally funded both bottom-up in private enterprises and top-down by major government bodies such as the EU (e.g. the European Green Deal).

One prescient industry leader is the Energy Web Foundation. Co-founded in 2017 by The Rocky Mountain Institute and Grid Singularity, this Swiss non-profit foundation now works closely with an energy ecosystem of partners including Blok Z, FlexiDAO, Electron and LO3 Energy (which moved its Pando project to the Energy Web Chain last year). The Foundation has deployed a public blockchain, with its validator nodes run by a federation of respected energy-corporations. This is refreshingly contrary to many energy projects utilizing private chains — something outside the scope of the Web3 ethos.

Carbon Market as Environmental Entry Gate

Today, carbon markets are already a reality. Examples include the EU Emissions Trading System and the Regional Greenhouse Gas Initiative (RGGI) — a mandatory cap and trade program in the northeastern US. As a clear and identifiable cause of global warming, CO2 is the only anthropic pressure that has been quantified financially with a broad market implementation. It is not surprising then that many Web3 projects are innovating the carbon value chain, as blockchain inherently enhances transparency, traceability, programmability, and tradability of carbon offsets.

A handful of enterprises are openly focusing on carbon markets. One well-developed solution thus far is Nori, which allows users to create and exchange Nori Carbon Removal Tonnes, each representing one CO2 tonne sequestrated for a minimum of ten years.

Moving beyond implementation forerunners, other carbon market projects include Toucan Protocol, which is building and integrating multiple key primitives for effective CO2 tokenization. This includes a carbon bridge to tokenize off-chain carbon offsets into on-chain tokenized carbon tonnes (TCO2), and a Pool mechanism, where TCO2 meeting specific quality requirements can be deposited and minted into fungible ERC-20 tokens. This makes price discovery fairer while accounting for carbon asset diversity.

The Klima DAO project — tenderly dubbed the “black hole for carbon” — deserves special mention. Forked from Olympus DAO’s game-theoretic bond issuance tokenomics, the project takes the risky gamble to create a decentrally governed cryptocurrency totally backed by carbon assets. This type of bold, open-source decentralized finance experimentation may help address key issues in today’s carbon value chain, such as scaling voluntary carbon markets.

Some projects have focused on the subject of pollution produced by blockchains themselves. For example, Celo, Brokoli, and Rubix Network are all pursuing carbon neutrality. The former two do so by offsetting user transactions. Celo, in particular, frames itself as a “carbon negative blockchain,” purchasing enough Wren carbon offsets to make the chain eight times carbon negative. The latter utilizes “Proof-of-Harvest,” a consensus mechanism where nodes do not store all transactions of all other nodes, reducing overall energy footprint. Chia Network has put forward “Proof-of-Space,” where “farmers” (miners) win blocks by keeping unused storage in their hardware.

Finally, projects like Regen Network and Rebalance Earth do not explicitly frame themselves as carbon market tooling, but in reality prioritize carbon as the de facto environmental unit of account. This being said, Regen Network’s ambition extends beyond carbon; projects like the CarbonPlus Grasslands strive to empower local farmers with new regenerative business models. Rebalance Earth — which still appears to be in the ideation phase — aims to assign biodiversity an economic value for ecosystem services, while also utilizing carbon as a unit of account. Tokenizing important species like the elephant will ideally allow them to leverage value for more effective conservation. This project article outlines that such value is mostly derived from the elephant’s carbon offsetting capacity.

Environmental Protection and Regeneration

Environmental protection and regeneration are sectors where Web3’s utility is less obvious. Disruptive opportunities still have yet to be identified, and corresponding solutions to be developed. Only a few enterprises frame themselves as part of this category, and those that do still tend to account with carbon.

The sector leader appears to be Regen Network, who aim to create real-world regenerative ecosystems through Web3 decentralized platform design (their community staking DAOs, for instance, blend architectural, tokenomic, and governance considerations). The team has proposed a handful of ecological state protocols to monitor and support ecosystem regeneration.

IXO strives towards building an “Internet of Impact” by identifying, verifying and tokenizing social or environmental impact data. To begin pursuing this goal, tests for a time-based community currency in Hong-Kong have been carried out, with more innovations forthcoming.

Projects like the Wood Tracking Protocol (WTP) make use of blockchain to authenticate the provenance of endangered natural resources. WTP tracks timber origin, and aims to replace current administrative systems, which have so far failed to prevent extensive illegal logging in Peru. These provenance protocols can be used for many protected species and other victims of poaching and overexploitation — e.g. Fishcoin, which traces fishery resources from fishing to consumption.

Generally, these Web3 environmental protection and regeneration seem to be terrestrially biased. Terrestrial bio- and eco-regions such as temperate forests and their corresponding fauna are much better understood, easier to gain attention for, and feel more urgent to restore. In contrast, major ecological subjects such as the seas, the oceans, their fauna, fungi and other non-woody plants have yet to be explored in Web3.

Finally, lesser known projects in this sector still deserve attention, take Decentralized Destination for example. Although still to be defined, the project’s core values promise to be original and remarkable, engaging with concepts such as impact measurement, re-localization, and sustainable tourism.

Moving Past Charitable Business Models

One of the main criticisms leveled at the Web3 environmental sector is the inability of projects to view the environment as anything other than a problem that can be solved by the goodwill of donors. This charity model I define as any entrepreneurial business model aimed at the common well-being that relies primarily on donors to ensure revenues. In line with this criticism, alternative Web3 models are moving forward:

  • Kolektivo is a Decentralized Exchange Trading System, a proposition for a new type of regenerative Web3 institution that aims to enable local populations to self-govern while engaging natural ecosystems they depend on. What’s particularly interesting about this institutional design is that it merges organizational patterns and learnings from DAOs and LETS, framing itself as a comprehensive adaptive governance tool.
  • Terra0: an early pioneer in environmental Web3, Terra0 traces the contours of a cybernetic forest capable of owning and using itself. It finances itself by selling licenses to humans through smart contracts to harvest forest yields, and makes use of its funds to maintain, protect, restore, or even to expand.

As mentioned earlier, many projects utilize the charity model. Without discussing them all at length: GainForest and Treejer work towards reforestation; Rewilder rewilds with funds collected through NFT sales. A number of ponzinomic charity coins copy SafeMoon’s tokenomics, such as Pangea Ocean Cleanup, SeaToken, World of Waves, CleanOcean, Aquari, Aquagoat, SafePanda and Bamboo. Because these charity coins are using funds collected to help environmental associations, they seem to be hijacking a problematic tokenomic design for a more noble purpose, but are not sustainable business models.

Emerging Climate Actors and Coalitions

Numerous industry-focused actors, aggregators, and coalitions are forming around the cryptosphere’s nascent environmental movement. Many of them hope to identify unique Web3 environmental projects and boost exposure. However, few groups have boots on the ground committed to large-scale regenerative pilots — which can be justified by the sector’s youth. Note that not all parties have similar aims and structures. We have…

  • Think-tanks like The Blockchain and Climate Institute.
  • Innovation programs like the #Hack4climate initiative.
  • Manifestos like The Crypto Climate Accord.
  • Non-profit organizations like the Blockchain for Climate Foundation, an attempt to put the Paris Agreement on the blockchain.

Finally, there are groups and coalitions like The Sovereign Nature Initiative, The Climate Chain Coalition, The Open Earth Foundation, The Climate Ledger Initiative or The Climate Collective; industry-oriented actor-networks rather than atomic projects. Among these coalitions, The Sovereign Nature Initiative stands out for the quality and originality of the projects it highlights and supports, as well as their philosophical praxis regarding the anthropocentric perception of nature. The Climate Collective is also of note as Celo’s ecosystem aims to redefine value creation by backing their reserve with natural capital currencies.

A Call to Action

Blockchain as a technological innovation is increasingly being utilized by the environmental sector, as it offers novel solutions to key anthropogenic issues such as climate change. Innovative and holistic approaches — adaptive, decentralized governance — are required to move past traditional environmental business models such as charitable giving. All of the Web3 projects presented here are inspiring avenues for multi-systemic technological, ecological, and economic innovation, which are only made possible by these new technologies.

Nonetheless, there is substantial room for improvement. At the heart of these existing Web3 projects which aim to protect and and regenerate the biosphere, the emphasis remains on terrestrial animals and woody plant species. Alternatives focusing on marine environments, fungi, insects, or other non-living climatic events must be pursued en masse, as they are in many ways more important! Questions of phytoplankton, seagrass beds in the Mediterranean, and melting permafrost are central subjects to climate change.
